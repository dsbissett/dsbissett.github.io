Robot Walks.

A robot traces circular arcs across the plane — each one 1/n of a revolution, swept either clockwise or counter-clockwise on the robot's free choice. After m arcs, how many distinct journeys close back to the start? Notes on the algorithm that answers this modulo a prime K.

n ∈ (1, 10] · n < m · n²·m ≤ 2·10⁴ · K prime, 10⁹ < K < 2·10⁹
§
§ One

The problem, in plain prose.

At every step, the robot has exactly two choices: swing its arc clockwise, or counter-clockwise. No abrupt heading changes, no turning in place — the heading flows smoothly through each arc, rotating by precisely 2π/n radians. We start facing North, and we want to count every distinct sequence of m arcs that closes the trajectory back to the origin.

For n = 3 and m = 6, only eight such journeys exist. For n = 5 and m = 25, more than seventy thousand. The numbers explode quickly, which is why the answer is reported modulo a chosen prime K.

Two seemingly small twists make this hard. First, the path's closure is a geometric constraint on the sum of displacement vectors — not a simple graph property. Second, an arc may be traversed many times in either direction, so brute enumeration is hopeless even for modest m.

§ Two

A graph, hiding inside the geometry.

The robot looks like a continuous machine, but its discrete shadow is a graph. Because every arc rotates the heading by exactly 2π/n, only n distinct headings ever occur. The state space — though embedded in the plane — collapses onto a directed multigraph with the shape of a cycle Cn.

Each arc, depending on whether it is clockwise or counter-clockwise, becomes an edge between adjacent headings. The robot's journey is a walk on this cycle. We describe a walk by a multiplicity vector  c = (c0, c1, …, cn−1), counting how many arcs the robot traces in each edge class. Of course, Σci = m.

Geometric closure becomes a symmetry constraint.

For the robot to come home, the sum of its arc displacements must vanish. By the n-fold rotational symmetry of the arc family, this translates into a clean combinatorial condition on c: the multiplicities must be invariant under a specific rotation of the cycle. The form of this condition is the reason every n gets its own enumeration loop.

Left: the robot's arc-trace in the plane. Right: its discrete shadow — a directed cycle C5 with parallel edges in both directions. The solid black edges are clockwise arcs (heading decreases by 2π/5); dashed vermillion are counter-clockwise (heading increases).

§ Three

The BEST theorem.

For a connected directed multigraph in which every vertex has equal in- and out-degree, the count of Eulerian circuits — walks that traverse every edge exactly once — has a beautiful closed form. It is one of the few "exact formulas" in combinatorics that delivers an instant huge number.

ec(G) = Tw(G) · ∏ v ∈ V (deg+v − 1)!
Eulerian circuits = arborescences rooted at w  ×  product of (out-degree − 1)! over all vertices

Tw(G) counts the spanning arborescences (in-trees) rooted at any chosen vertex w — remarkably, this number is the same for every w. The other factor multiplies, at each vertex, the orderings of the outgoing edges after the first.

For this problem, the cycle graph has wonderful structure: its arborescences have a clean polynomial expression. Picking the root to be the start vertex, an arborescence is fully determined by choosing where on the cycle the "missing" edge sits — one summand per cycle position. The algorithm computes this sum directly, in O(n) per evaluation.

Splitting an undirected count into direction-aware parts.

Our ci counts total traversals of edge class i, mixing clockwise and counter-clockwise. To apply BEST, we split each into directional counts:  xi clockwise, yi counter-clockwise, with xi + yi = ci.

A subtle conservation law lurks here: for the directed walk to close, the net angular drift δ = xi − yi must be the same for every edge of the cycle. (If it weren't, the in- and out-degrees would mismatch somewhere.) So the directional split is governed by a single integer δ, ranging in steps of two from −min(c) up to +min(c).

That single parameter is the loop variable t inside every count_n* function. Each iteration plugs in one value of δ, computes the BEST count, and adds it to the running total.

§ Four

The solution, in motion.

Three views of the very same count — n = 3, m = 6, answer 8. First the robot traces each closing journey in the plane; then those journeys become Eulerian circuits on the cycle C3; finally the kernel's drift sum recovers the total 1 + 6 + 1 directly. The three are one story told three ways.

§ Five

The algorithm, four passes.

With the theorem in hand, the solver is a careful piece of bookkeeping. Every quantity lives in ℤ/Kℤ, so factorials, inverse factorials and modular inverses are precomputed once and reused throughout. Then we sum over multiplicity vectors and drifts.

  1. Precompute — modular machinery up to m.

    Build three arrays: fact[i] = i! mod K; invfact[i] = (i!)−1; and invint[i] = i−1. The first uses a forward loop, the second a backward pass starting from fermat(fact[m]), and the third the classical recurrence inv(i) = ⌊K/i⌋·inv(K mod i).

    Cost: O(m). One-time.

  2. Enumerate the admissible multiplicity vectors.

    For each n ∈ {2, …, 10}, the geometric-closure constraint forces a specific symmetry on c. The outer loops in solve_n* walk this constrained lattice, parameterising it by a few free integers. Most n admit only one or two "shapes" of c; even n = 6, 10 need a parity flag to handle odd m.

    Cost: O(m⌈n/4⌉) where k = ⌈n/4⌉ free parameters.

  3. Evaluate count_n* for each vector.

    The inner kernel sums over the drift δ. For each δ it forms three pieces: the vertex factor (product of factorials of edge-pair sums at each vertex), the multinomial d (counting orderings of xi CW and yi CCW arcs), and the tree polynomial (BEST's arborescence count for the cycle).

    The multinomial is maintained incrementally across drifts: as δ grows by 2, each xi ticks up by 1 and each yi ticks down. The new d is the old one multiplied by yi and divided by xi+1 — hence the invint table.

    Cost: O(min(c) · n) per kernel call.

  4. Accumulate modulo K.

    Every multiplication carries an immediate % K. The final result is the sum over all (vector, drift) pairs of (vertex × multinomial × tree). Print it.

What the inner kernel looks like, distilled.

// for each net drift δ in steps of 2 from −min(c) to +min(c):
for (i64 t = 0; t < steps; t++) {
    // (1) Tree polynomial − BEST's arborescence count on the cycle
    //     Σ over cut-position k of ∏(x's on one side) · ∏(y's on the other)
    i64 tree = sx1;
    tree = (tree + p1 * sx2) % K;
    tree = (tree + p2 * sx3) % K;
    // ... up to n terms ...
    tree = (tree + p_{n-1}) % K;

    // (2) Accumulate this drift's contribution
    total = (total + tree * d) % K;

    // (3) Slide δ → δ + 2: update multinomial d incrementally
    d = d * p_{n-1} % K * y_{n-1} % K;       // new y factors die off
    for (i in 0..n-1) {
        ++x[i]; d = d * invint[x[i]] % K;     // new x⁻¹ factors
        --y[i];
    }
}
return vertex * total % K;

Two threads run through this loop — one tracking the tree polynomial, the other the multinomial — and they share state. The bookkeeping is delicate enough that the kernel is unrolled by hand for each n, but the structure is identical in every case.

§ Six

Why every n is its own problem.

The geometric-closure constraint takes a different form for each n — depending on whether n is prime, composite, or shares a factor with 2. The dispatcher routes to one of nine specialised functions. The table below is the index.

2
Closed form
Half-circle arcs cancel in pairs: the count is the binomial coefficient C(m, m/2), zero when m is odd.
3
Uniform · m ≡ 0 (mod 3)
Every cycle edge is used the same number of times: ci = m/3. A single kernel call.
4
One free parameter
Opposite edges must balance: iterate a ∈ [0, m/2] with c = (a, m/2−a, a, m/2−a).
5
Uniform · m ≡ 0 (mod 5)
Like n = 3: all five multiplicities are equal to m/5.
6
Parity branch · flag f
Even and odd m behave differently. A flag f ∈ {0, 3} selects which sub-lattice of c-vectors to walk; both share the same kernel skeleton with a rotated index order.
7
Uniform · m ≡ 0 (mod 7)
A single kernel call with all ci = m/7.
8
Three free parameters
Triple loop over w0, w1, w2 with an extra parity index p; opposite edges constrained to match.
9
Two free parameters + parity
Threefold symmetry on the cycle: pick u0, u1, u2 with matching parity, repeat the triple three times around the cycle.
10
Four parameters · parity branch
The most general case: quadruple loop with a parity flag, the same shape as n = 6 but two scales bigger.

The pattern is suggestive: prime n reduce to a single uniform call, while composite n open up an interior of free parameters whose dimension grows roughly as n/4. This is why the constraint n²m ≤ 2·10⁴ hits hardest for n = 10: it pays for both the outer loop's volume and the inner kernel's drift sweep.

Complexity, briefly.

Precompute is O(m). The outer enumeration over c-vectors has O(m⌈n/4⌉) shapes for composite n, each calling a kernel that does O(n · min(c)) work. Worst case fits comfortably under n²m, which is exactly the bound the problem imposes — 2·104. Modular arithmetic adds a small constant factor and never any allocations after the precompute.

The whole solution is non-asymptotic. No DP table grows with n or m beyond the three precomputed arrays; every other quantity is a handful of 64-bit registers carried through the inner loop. The hot path is dominated by modular multiplications, of which there are roughly 12n²m across a worst-case test.
